Two bills, split into what's fixed and what grows

Each AWS service against the one that replaces it. AgentCore Runtime in front of AWS Fargate; AgentCore Memory in front of Amazon DynamoDB and the second Amazon Bedrock call our extractor makes every turn; AgentCore Gateway in front of in-process tool calls. The reply Bedrock call is identical on both engines and excluded from both. Every rate is quoted from a vendor pricing page; every quantity is counted from this repository; the usage assumptions are stated in full at the bottom.

Part one

This is the bill at 3 a.m. with zero users signed in. It does not move with traffic, and it is shared across every user you ever add. It is the whole reason AgentCore wins at small scale.

Read the two right-hand columns together, because they move in opposite directions. The ratio falls from 187× to 13.5× as you add users — that is Fargate's fixed $18 spreading across more people, and it is the honest shape of the thing. What grows without limit is the money: $19/month at one user, $1.28 M/month at a million — about $15.4 M a year. The floor is what hurts at small scale; the 13.5× per-turn gap is what hurts at large scale. Both favour AgentCore, for different reasons, which is why this page is split in two.

The trend: two straight lines that never cross, settling 13.5× apart

Both axes logarithmic, so a constant ratio is a constant vertical gap. The gap narrows over the first two decades — Fargate's fixed cost amortising — then locks at 13.5× and stays there. Engine A is above engine B at every user count on the chart.

The dashed verticals are where engine A has to add whole Fargate tasks to carry the evening peak — 4 tasks at 100 k users, 31 at a million. AgentCore Runtime has no steps: it bills only the seconds it runs, so its line is straight all the way across. At a million users the saving is $1.28 M a month, about $15.4 M a year.

The evening peak matters more than it looks. Fargate has to be sized for the busiest hour and is then billed for all 730, so a load concentrated into four hours a day wastes roughly five sixths of what you pay for it. At the user counts above one 0.5 vCPU task still covers the peak, which is why Fargate stays at $18.02 in the table rather than stepping up — but every step it eventually takes is another whole $18.02, whereas AgentCore Runtime has no steps at all.

Part four

Where AgentCore loses

Stated because it's true — and because it's what makes the rest believable

  • AgentCore Runtime is 2.20× dearer per hour than Fargate for identical resources ($0.0542 vs $0.0247 for 0.5 vCPU / 1 GiB). It wins on billing granularity, not unit price.
  • AgentCore Memory is ~124× dearer per turn than DynamoDB, and its storage line is 6.4× dearer. It loses the storage layer outright; DynamoDB on-demand is so cheap that beating it is worth almost nothing.
  • AgentCore Gateway is a line item engine A doesn't have at all. Engine A's tools run in-process for free inside a task it already pays for.
  • The Lambda behind AgentCore Gateway is not counted here. It bills its own requests and GB-seconds. Lambda's rates weren't sourced for this model, so engine B's figure is an understatement rather than its advantage an overstatement.
  • The advantage ratio shrinks as you grow — 187× at one user, 13.5× at ten thousand. Anyone quoting the small-scale number to a large-scale audience will be corrected.
  • This compares two architectures, not the current deployment. The repo today runs both engines side by side as a measurement harness, which costs more than either alone. It also does not remove Fargate — a same-sized proxy still fronts AgentCore — nor DynamoDB, which the Gateway's Lambda still reads and writes.
  • Latency is not on this page. No measurement of either engine exists; every duration in the repo is authored. Engine A makes up to 5 sequential Converse round-trips on a tool turn against engine B's one call, so the direction is genuinely unknown.
