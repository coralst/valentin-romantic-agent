Samantha keeps her preferences quiet and her opinions firm, which is a difficult combination to buy presents for. What I have learned across five years of your conversations is that she does not want to be surprised so much as she wants to be understood in advance — the difference being that the first one asks her to perform delight and the second one saves her the trouble.

She met you at a pottery class on a rainy Sunday, and you both made the same lopsided mug. That detail does more work than any of the twenty other fields I hold: it explains the handmade-over-expensive instinct, the glaze set at the top of her wish list, and why the linen apron is a better idea than the necklace.

What I would ask her next

  1. “What's your father's brother called?” Closes one of the two gaps on her family tree.
  2. “Is there a glaze colour you keep running out of?” Turns the wish list into something you can actually buy.
  3. “Which anniversary do you two actually celebrate?” I hold two dates, eleven days apart, and I have been guessing.

Where I am guessing

Eleven of the things I believe about her are inferences rather than facts she has told you — that she would rather have one good thing than three nice ones, that flowers are fine as long as they are not roses, that handmade beats expensive every time. They are in the file marked as guesses, and any one of them takes ten seconds to confirm.
